What happens to a cattle microbiome in a human world?

An integrative multi-omics study of domestic and stray cattle to understand how human contact, shared environments, diet, and veterinary care shape gut microbes, microbial activity, host response, and public-health risk.

200cattle across domestic and stray populations, sampled across three climatic regions

Two ways of living
beside us.

The study uses cattle as a living model for a bigger One Health question: how do human environments alter animal microbiomes, and what might that mean for people?

Domestic cattle

Managed proximity

Raised in farming systems with regular human contact, controlled diets, veterinary care, and possible antibiotic exposure.

  • Close, repeated contact with people
  • Structured feed and housing
  • Routine veterinary intervention
Stray cattle

Unmanaged proximity

Living freely in human settlements, exposed to urban environments and often relying on scavenged food and waste.

  • Shared streets and public spaces
  • Variable food and environmental exposure
  • Minimal or irregular veterinary care
Between them is the signal.By comparing both populations, the study looks for a measurable “humanization” signature—microbial communities that reflect human-associated environments.

One question.
Six linked datasets.

The proposal remains centred on microbiome humanization, while adding the measurements needed to follow the story from exposure to function and host response.

01 / MICROBIOME

Who is there?

Map bacterial, archaeal, viral, and eukaryotic communities through whole-genome shotgun metagenomics.

02 / MICROBIAL ACTIVITY

What are they doing?

Use microbial metatranscriptomics in a smaller subset to compare active pathways with the genes that are merely present.

03 / HOST RESPONSE

How does the host respond?

Use blood or PBMC RNA-seq to profile immune, inflammatory, metabolic, and epithelial-barrier-related expression.

04 / DIETARY PLANTS

What entered the system?

Use fecal plant DNA metabarcoding to track supplied fodder, grasses, weeds, crop residues, and food-associated plant material.

05 / RESISTOME

What can they resist?

Screen metagenomic reads against CARD, ResFinder, and MEGARes to compare resistance genes and their mobility.

06 / PARASITES

What else is present?

Combine microscopy and molecular confirmation to measure macroparasite diversity and potential zoonotic risk.

Plant / diet exposure → gut microbiome → microbial gene activity → host gene expressionThis is the added layer of the same PhD story: not a separate plant-science project, but a way to explain why the microbiome differs and what those differences do.

Research pathway

From field sample to usable evidence.

The workflow is designed to move from careful collection to analysis that can support health surveillance, livestock management, and policy.

01

Collect

Fresh fecal samples from 100 domestic and 100 stray cattle, with urban and rural contexts represented.

02

Sequence

Extract high-quality DNA and use paired-end whole-genome shotgun sequencing for broad community coverage.

03

Profile function and exposure

Measure diversity, taxonomic composition, dietary plant signatures, functional pathways, resistance genes, and parasite load between groups.

04

Read the host response

Use a smaller matched subset—about 15–20 cattle per group—for microbial metatranscriptomics and host blood or PBMC RNA-seq.

05

Model the chain

Use multivariate statistics and machine learning to connect plant exposure, microbiome composition, microbial activity, host response, and zoonotic risk.

How samples
become evidence.

Each method answers a different part of the same question. The result is supported when independent measurements point to the same pattern.

01 / FIELD DESIGN

Collect comparable samples

Sample 100 domestic and 100 stray cattle across urban and rural settings, recording management, antibiotic exposure, human contact, location, and visible health information.

02 / METAGENOMICS

Measure what is present

Extract DNA from feces and use shotgun sequencing. Quality-filter reads, remove bovine DNA, then classify organisms and annotate genes and pathways.

03 / METATRANSCRIPTOMICS

Measure what is active

In a smaller matched subset, sequence microbial RNA to test whether pathways such as carbohydrate breakdown, SCFA production, methane metabolism, stress response, or AMR are being expressed.

04 / HOST RNA-SEQ

Measure the animal response

Use blood or PBMC RNA-seq from about 15–20 cattle per group to compare immune, inflammatory, metabolic, and epithelial-barrier gene expression.

05 / PLANT DNA

Measure dietary exposure

Use chloroplast markers such as trnL, rbcL, or matK to identify fodder, grasses, weeds, crop residues, and human food-associated plants in fecal samples.

06 / PARASITES + AMR

Measure co-risk

Combine microscopy, parasite PCR, and resistome screening against CARD, ResFinder, and MEGARes to map parasite burden and resistance-gene profiles.

Why this matters
outside the lab.

The goal is not only to describe microbial difference, but to make the relationship between animal health, human health, and environment easier to act on.

Public health

Identify microbial and genetic markers that can sharpen zoonotic disease and antimicrobial resistance surveillance.

Better stewardship

Show how management and antibiotic exposure shape the cattle resistome, supporting more targeted interventions.

One Health policy

Provide evidence for livestock management, urban planning, and future longitudinal studies across regions.

The central aim

To find the microbial fingerprints of human proximity—and understand what they activate in the animal.

This proposal builds a comparative baseline for studying cattle that live with people in very different ways, linking dietary exposure to microbial function and host response.
